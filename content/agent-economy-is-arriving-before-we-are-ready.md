# The agent economy is arriving before trust is ready

I like the idea of useful AI agents. I really do.

Not the theatrical version where an agent performs a suspiciously perfect demo and everyone pretends the seven failed attempts do not count. I mean the ordinary version: an assistant that can gather the right sources, draft something useful, compare options, update a system, prepare a purchase, or turn a messy pile of notes into something I can actually review.

That is where the value is. Less tab-hopping. Less admin sludge. Less "I know what I want, but I now have to spend forty minutes arranging the software around the thing I want."

But the direction of travel is also very clear now. Agents are not going to stay inside chat boxes. They are going to touch money, files, tools, creative software, shopping carts, calendars, customer systems, and trading rails. The latest official releases from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are all framed around work loops rather than just answers. Stripe is already pushing payment infrastructure that can support agent-mediated buying flows. Research is appearing around language-model agents operating under real capital. Creative assistants are moving into design and production tools.

That is useful. It is also where I start getting twitchy.

## The problem is not autonomy

I do not think the problem is "AI agents are too autonomous". That is too blunt.

The problem is that autonomy often arrives before the user interface for trust.

If an agent can do something on my behalf, I need to understand five things quickly:

1. What can it do?
2. What can it not do?
3. What has it already done?
4. What will it ask me before doing?
5. How do I undo or stop it?

Most agent demos are excellent at the first question and vague on the other four. They show capability. They do not always show boundaries.

That is the bit I care about most. Boundaries are not anti-automation. They are what make automation usable for normal people who cannot spend all day reading logs.

## Payment changes the whole feeling

An agent that can recommend a product is one thing.

An agent that can buy it is another.

That does not mean agent payments are bad. In fact, I can see the appeal immediately. If I trust a system, why should it not renew a domain, buy printer ink, top up a subscription, book the cheapest sensible travel option, or handle boring purchases within a budget?

The catch is that "within a budget" is doing a lot of work.

I would want rules like:

- never spend over a set amount without approval
- never start a subscription without approval
- never buy from unknown merchants without approval
- show me the final basket before checkout
- keep a receipt I can understand
- let me cancel the action easily

That sounds obvious, but a lot of tech fails at obvious once growth pressure arrives. The temptation will be to make the agent feel smoother by removing friction. Sometimes that will be helpful. Sometimes the friction is the safety rail.

## The trust layer needs to be visible

The best agent systems will probably feel less magical than the worst demos.

They will show drafts. They will show receipts. They will say when evidence is weak. They will separate "I can prepare this" from "I can send this". They will let you approve a class of low-risk actions while still stopping on anything expensive, public, or hard to reverse.

That is what I want from my own workflow as well. I do not want agents publishing posts, sending newsletters, spending money, or changing websites without review. Drafts are good. Queues are good. Approval gates are good. They are not bureaucracy. They are how the work stays mine.

I think that is going to be the bigger divide in AI products over the next year: not just which model is cleverest, but which systems make users feel properly in control.

## My current test

If I am evaluating an agent product now, I am less impressed by "look what it can do" than I used to be.

My first questions are:

- Can I see what it touched?
- Can I see why it chose that action?
- Can I stop it?
- Can I limit it?
- Can I review before anything public happens?
- Can I undo the result?

If the answer is no, then the agent is still a demo to me, even if it looks polished.

The agent economy is coming. I think it will be genuinely useful. But the winning products will not be the ones that make agents feel like they can do anything. They will be the ones that make it clear what the agent is allowed to do, what it has done, and where the human still has the final word.

That is not a lack of ambition. That is the only version of ambition I actually trust.
